
# Modulation Schemes in the Wi‑Fi PHY Layer

## Introduction

Modulation is the process of converting digital data into electromagnetic waveforms that can be transmitted over the wireless medium. In IEEE 802.11 WLAN systems, modulation is implemented in the Physical Layer (PHY) and directly impacts:

- Throughput
- Spectral efficiency
- Reliability
- Range
- Signal robustness
- Power efficiency

Wi‑Fi standards evolved from simple single‑carrier modulation techniques to highly sophisticated multi‑carrier OFDM and OFDMA systems combined with advanced coding and MIMO technologies.

---

## Fundamentals of Modulation

Wireless communication transmits information by altering properties of a carrier signal:

- Amplitude
- Frequency
- Phase

Different modulation schemes encode bits differently:

| Modulation | Bits per Symbol |
|---|---|
| BPSK | 1 |
| QPSK | 2 |
| 16‑QAM | 4 |
| 64‑QAM | 6 |
| 256‑QAM | 8 |
| 1024‑QAM | 10 |
| 4096‑QAM | 12 |

Higher‑order modulation increases throughput but requires better Signal‑to‑Noise Ratio (SNR).

---

## Common Wi‑Fi Modulation Schemes

### 1. BPSK (Binary Phase Shift Keying)

BPSK changes the carrier phase between two states.

Characteristics:
- 1 bit per symbol
- Highly robust against noise
- Used in low data rate control frames
- Excellent long‑range reliability

Advantages:
- Very low BER
- Strong resistance to interference

Disadvantages:
- Lowest throughput

---

### 2. QPSK (Quadrature Phase Shift Keying)

QPSK uses four phase states.

Characteristics:
- 2 bits per symbol
- Better spectral efficiency than BPSK
- Moderate robustness

Applications:
- Mid‑range data transmission
- Reliable WLAN links

---

### 3. 16‑QAM

Quadrature Amplitude Modulation combines amplitude and phase variations.

Characteristics:
- 4 bits per symbol
- Increased throughput
- Requires cleaner channel conditions

---

### 4. 64‑QAM

Characteristics:
- 6 bits per symbol
- Common in 802.11n/ac systems
- High throughput capability

Requirements:
- High SNR
- Low interference environment

---

### 5. 256‑QAM

Characteristics:
- 8 bits per symbol
- Used extensively in Wi‑Fi 5 and Wi‑Fi 6
- Significant increase in spectral efficiency

Challenges:
- Sensitive to noise
- Requires advanced RF design

---

### 6. 1024‑QAM

Characteristics:
- 10 bits per symbol
- Introduced in Wi‑Fi 6
- Very high throughput

Requirements:
- Excellent channel conditions
- High SINR
- Short‑range deployment

---

### 7. 4096‑QAM

Characteristics:
- 12 bits per symbol
- Used in Wi‑Fi 7
- Extremely high spectral efficiency

Challenges:
- Extremely sensitive to interference
- Requires premium RF calibration and beamforming

---

## OFDM-Based Modulation

Modern Wi‑Fi standards use OFDM (Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing).

In OFDM:
- The channel is divided into multiple orthogonal subcarriers
- Each subcarrier can carry modulated symbols independently
- FFT/IFFT operations generate orthogonal carriers

Benefits:
- High spectral efficiency
- Multipath resistance
- Better performance in indoor environments

## Modulation Across Wi‑Fi Generations

| Wi‑Fi Standard | PHY Technique | Maximum Modulation | Channel Width |
|---|---|---|---|
| 802.11b | DSSS/CCK | QPSK/CCK | 20 MHz |
| 802.11a | OFDM | 64‑QAM | 20 MHz |
| 802.11g | OFDM | 64‑QAM | 20 MHz |
| 802.11n | OFDM + MIMO | 64‑QAM | 20/40 MHz |
| 802.11ac | OFDM + MU‑MIMO | 256‑QAM | 20/40/80/160 MHz |
| 802.11ax | OFDMA + MU‑MIMO | 1024‑QAM | Up to 160 MHz |
| 802.11be | OFDMA + Multi‑Link | 4096‑QAM | Up to 320 MHz |

---

## Adaptive Modulation and Coding (AMC)

Wi‑Fi dynamically changes modulation based on channel conditions.

Factors considered:
- SNR
- RSSI
- Packet Error Rate
- Interference
- Distance

Example:
- Weak signal → BPSK/QPSK
- Strong signal → 1024‑QAM

This mechanism improves overall network efficiency.

## Real‑World Considerations

Enterprise WLAN engineers consider:
- Client capability
- RF interference
- Cell overlap
- Channel utilization
- Distance
- Multipath fading

High‑order modulation performs best:
- Near APs
- In low‑interference environments
- With beamforming enabled

---

## Conclusion

Modulation schemes form the foundation of Wi‑Fi PHY layer communication. Modern Wi‑Fi standards continuously improve throughput by adopting higher‑order modulation and combining it with OFDM, MIMO, and OFDMA technologies. While higher‑order modulation dramatically improves spectral efficiency, it also increases sensitivity to channel impairments, requiring sophisticated RF engineering and adaptive rate selection algorithms.
